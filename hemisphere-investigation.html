Who Benefits From the Chaos?

Is America building a new economic order in the Western Hemisphere? A growing network of resource agreements offers a way to test the theory against signed policy, actual production and the cost of strategic independence.

EP NEWS INVESTIGATIONS · OCTOBER 28, 2026 · ONGOING

Trade confrontations, energy disruptions, industrial investments and shifting alliances often appear to be separate stories. The Trump administration has supplied a documented strategic framework that may connect some of them: its Trump Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine, formally articulated in the December 2025 National Security Strategy and reinforced in the January 2026 National Defense Strategy.

The administration explicitly seeks to protect American access to strategic geography and prevent non-hemispheric rivals from controlling vital regional assets. But whether its policies are creating a self-sufficient hemisphere—and whether international disruptions were caused by that strategy—remain distinct, testable questions. Congressional Research Service has documented both regional policy shifts and pushback from neighboring governments.

The question is not whether every crisis was planned. It is whether the United States is gaining durable economic leverage from a changing global order—and who pays for that leverage.

The resource agreements: a regional supply-chain strategy

The United States need not mine or manufacture every essential input domestically to reduce dependence on suppliers outside the Americas. A mineral mined in South America, processed in North America and used by an American manufacturer may strengthen regional resilience—provided ownership, processing, logistics and costs are also accounted for.

Several new agreements give that possibility a concrete diplomatic foundation. Yet a cooperation framework is not a shipment; a financing ceiling is not disbursed capital; and access to a deposit is not an operating refinery.

The September joint statement on hemispheric sovereignty, involving the United States and 14 regional governments, explicitly includes diversifying critical-mineral sourcing and protecting strategic infrastructure. That provides a broader regional context for the bilateral announcements, though it is not itself a binding supply contract.

New supply versus shifting existing supply

Two different outcomes must be counted separately. New production adds capacity through new mines, processing plants or energy facilities. Supplier substitution redirects existing purchases from outside the hemisphere to regional partners. Both could reduce concentration risk, but neither automatically increases American manufacturing employment or lowers consumer prices.

Critical minerals present a particular challenge: moving extraction into the Americas does little to reduce dependence if refining or essential components remain controlled by firms or facilities elsewhere. EP News will track the entire chain, including mineral origin, refining country, ownership, offtake agreements, logistics, prices and actual U.S. deliveries.

What measurable outcomes tell us

U.S. LNG exports averaged 17.4 billion cubic feet per day in the first half of 2026, 23% above the comparable 2025 period. This is real output and potentially valuable leverage, but the capacity involved was developed over years and cannot simply be credited to recent geopolitical events.

Meanwhile, September durable-goods orders rose 1.8%, with transportation equipment accounting for most of the increase. That measures demand, not proof of tariff-driven reshoring. And JPMorganChase's more than $230 billion in reported financing and facilitated transactions is not a count of completed plants or new jobs.

The complication: regional partners have options

A strategy intended to consolidate hemispheric influence may also encourage neighboring countries to diversify away from U.S. dependence. Canada's October 2026 UAE trade agreement illustrates this tension. The agreement is signed but not yet in force; its negotiation and investment history predates the latest U.S. trade disputes. It cannot be attributed entirely to Washington's policies. Still, it shows why regional integration should be measured rather than presumed.

Who benefits—and who pays?

Potential beneficiaries include energy exporters, mineral producers, manufacturers, financiers and governments seeking more reliable access to strategic resources. Costs may fall on households, tariff-paying importers, taxpayers underwriting financing, affected industries and partner countries confronting sovereignty or trade-offs. Some gains can be shared; others may be redistributed.

The investigation will therefore follow five tests: (1) agreements signed versus projects operating; (2) new production versus redirected sourcing; (3) imports from outside the hemisphere versus regional sourcing, including processing; (4) jobs, prices, taxpayer exposure and diplomatic costs; and (5) outcomes compared with preexisting trends.

The working conclusion

The Trump Corollary is documented U.S. policy. The resource agreements are documented diplomatic actions. Some energy and manufacturing changes are measurable. Together they establish a serious case for investigating a broader hemispheric economic strategy.

They do not yet prove that the hemisphere is becoming self-sufficient, that every international disruption is connected, or that the benefits exceed the costs. Those are the questions EP News will continue to test as agreements turn—or fail to turn—into production, deliveries and lasting economic leverage.
