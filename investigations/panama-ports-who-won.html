WHO BENEFITS FROM THE CHAOS? · INVESTIGATION

China Lost Control of Two Panama Canal Ports. But Who Actually Won?

Investigative analysis based on official records and independent reporting. Terminal throughput is not the same as Panama Canal transit traffic. Arbitration demands are disputed claims, not paid liabilities.

PANAMA CHANGED THE OPERATORS AT TWO CANAL PORTS. WHO HAS GAINED?

A change in control at the entrances to the Panama Canal has produced a strategic result Washington wanted: a Hong Kong-linked operator no longer runs two important container terminals. But new cargo figures show that Balboa and Cristóbal have lost business while other Panamanian ports have gained it. That is a measurable reshuffling, not yet proof of a net American economic or security gain.

The legal change is real. Panama's Supreme Court ruled on January 29, 2026, that the law underpinning Panama Ports Company's concession was unconstitutional. Panama's own September 30 disclosure filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission states that the ruling became final on February 23 and that the government assumed control of Balboa and Cristóbal. (Panama SEC disclosure: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/76027/000119312526408721/d135474dex99d.htm )

That day, Panama approved temporary concessions of up to 18 months: APM Terminals, part of Danish group A.P. Moller-Maersk, for Balboa on the Pacific side; and TIL Panamá, associated with Mediterranean Shipping Company, for Cristóbal on the Atlantic side. APM independently confirmed it began temporary operations at Balboa on February 24. (Panama presidency, February 23: https://www.presidencia.gob.pa/publicacion/consejo-de-gabinete-aprueba-dos-contratos-de-concesion-transitorios-que-garantizan-la-operacion-de-los-puertos-cristobal-y-balboa- ; APM Terminals, February 24: https://www2.apmterminals.com/en/news/news-releases/2026/260224-APM-Terminals-begins-temporary-operations-Port-of-Balboa )

The terminals are not the canal itself. The Panama Canal Authority administers the waterway; the dispute concerns container-port concessions at its entrances. Nor have the ports become U.S.-owned. Their temporary operators are European-linked companies, while the Panamanian state retains sovereignty and authority to award new concessions. The distinction matters when evaluating political claims that America has "taken back" the canal. (Panama SEC disclosure: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/76027/000119312526408721/d135474dex99d.htm ; Associated Press, February 23: https://apnews.com/article/110af98b3782a08c242ecb5edb512614 )

WHAT THE TRAFFIC FIGURES SHOW

Panama Maritime Authority's official October 14 statistics, corroborated by October 20-21 independent reporting, show the national port system moved 7,605,946 twenty-foot-equivalent units (TEUs) in January-September 2026, up 3.2% from 7,368,837 a year earlier. But Balboa moved 1,769,804 TEUs, down 10.8%, and Cristóbal moved 751,558, down 20.4%. Together, the two transferred terminals handled 407,683 fewer TEUs than in January-September 2025, while Panama's national port system handled 237,109 more TEUs. These differences are calculated from the Maritime Authority's published totals; they are not a traced accounting of where individual cargo shipments went. (AMP official F-195, October 14: https://www.amp.gob.pa/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/F-195-Movimiento-de-Contenedores-en-el-SPN-en-TEU-1.pdf ; Infobae, October 20 corroboration: https://www.infobae.com/panama/2026/10/20/puertos-de-panama-superan-los-76-millones-de-teu-hasta-septiembre-pese-a-las-caidas-de-balboa-y-cristobal/?outputType=amp-type ; Splash247, October 21: https://splash247.com/panamas-former-hutchison-terminals-suffer-double-digit-volume-declines/ )

Other ports gained: Manzanillo International Terminal handled 2,445,560 TEUs, up 18.7%; Colón Container Terminal handled 1,465,465, up 14.8%; and PSA Panama International Terminal handled 1,133,181, up 9.8%. The numbers support a redistribution of business within Panama, not the claim that the entire national port system collapsed. They also do not prove that all traffic lost by Balboa or Cristóbal moved to those specific terminals; a matched shipping-line and origin-destination analysis is still needed. (Infobae, October 20: https://www.infobae.com/panama/2026/10/20/puertos-de-panama-superan-los-76-millones-de-teu-hasta-septiembre-pese-a-las-caidas-de-balboa-y-cristobal/?outputType=amp-type )

A separate measure is moving in the other direction. The Panama Canal Authority said the waterway handled 13,951 vessel transits in fiscal 2026, up 4.1% from 13,404 in fiscal 2025, and 521 million CP/SUAB tons, up 6.5%. These are canal transit and vessel-measurement figures for October-September, not January-September container handling at Balboa and Cristóbal. They cannot be substituted for terminal throughput or attributed to the concession change. The authority also cited favorable water levels and shifting trade flows. (Canal Authority, October 2026: https://pancanal.com/canal-de-panama-registra-aumento-de-transitos-y-tonelaje-durante-el-ano-fiscal-2026/ )

WHO GAINS — AND WHO PAYS?

Panama can benefit if a new tender improves concession revenue, transparency and competition. The temporary concessions reportedly call for $26.1 million from APMT and $15.8 million from TIL, or $41.9 million combined over up to 18 months. Those are contract amounts, not verified cash collections or net fiscal profit. The final contract schedules, receipts and operating costs still require reconciliation. (La Prensa, February 23: https://www.prensa.com/economia/se-oficializan-contratos-y-contraprestaciones-economicas-con-apm-terminals-y-til-para-operar-puertos-de-balboa-y-cristobal/ )

The United States may gain from reduced dependence on a Hong Kong-based operator at a strategic chokepoint and from more diverse logistics providers. That is a plausible security benefit, not a quantified result. The January 2026 National Defense Strategy explicitly names guaranteed U.S. commercial and military access to the Panama Canal as a priority of the Trump Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine. It does not establish that the port ruling caused additional American shipments, reduced U.S. freight costs, or increased U.S. control of the canal. (2026 National Defense Strategy: https://media.defense.gov/2026/Jan/23/2003864773/-1/-1/0/2026-NATIONAL-DEFENSESTRATEGY.PDF )

Panamanian workers face a more immediate cost. On October 13, Panama's Labor Ministry announced measures to secure Serviestiba assets, bank accounts and receivables after the port staffing firm said it would close for economic reasons. Reporting identified 1,386 affected workers. Officials were discussing hiring arrangements with the new operators, but the number ultimately re-employed and the full severance bill were not established in the documents reviewed. This is not proof that all 1,386 workers were laid off permanently. (Labor Ministry, October 13: https://www.mitradel.gob.pa/mitradel-ejecuta-medidas-para-proteger-empleo-y-derechos-laborales-de-trabajadores-portuarios/ ; La Prensa, October 13: https://www.prensa.com/economia/mitradel-ordena-secuestro-de-cuentas-de-serviestiba/ )

There is also a potentially substantial legal bill. Panama's SEC disclosure reports that Panama Ports Company seeks more than $2 billion in a contract arbitration, while parent CK Hutchison initiated a distinct investment-treaty arbitration on August 20 claiming more than $1.5 billion. These are contested demands, not adjudicated debts or amounts paid. They should not simply be added into a taxpayer-loss total because the proceedings have different parties and legal bases and potential overlap has not been evaluated. (Panama SEC disclosure: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/76027/000119312526408721/d135474dex99d.htm ; CK Hutchison statement, August 20: https://www.ckh.com.hk/en/media/press_each.php?id=3480 )

China has not treated the dispute as routine port administration. Reuters reported on March 10 that COSCO Shipping suspended operations at Balboa, citing a customer notice, without establishing whether the suspension was permanent. The Financial Times reported that Chinese officials pressed Maersk and MSC to leave the interim operations. These are material foreign responses, but they do not establish that Beijing controlled the Panama Canal itself. (Reuters, March 10: https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinas-cosco-shipping-suspends-operations-panamas-balboa-port-la-prensa-media-2026-03-10/ ; Financial Times, April 2026: https://www.ft.com/content/91d60b9e-374d-4dc3-bd57-3cfb3221f400 )

THE DEAL THAT DID NOT CLOSE

In March 2025, CK Hutchison proposed selling a broad portfolio of global ports, including its Panama interests, to a consortium involving BlackRock and MSC. Reporting valued the global transaction at roughly $23 billion. It was a proposed multi-country transaction, not a $23 billion investment in Panama or a completed U.S. acquisition of Balboa and Cristóbal. The 2026 court ruling and international objections complicated the sale; a final completed transfer of these two concessions to that consortium has not been verified. (Reuters, March 4, 2025: https://www.reuters.com/markets/deals/ck-hutchison-sell-80-stake-hutchison-ports-group-1777-billion-deal-2025-03-04/ ; Reuters, January 30, 2026: https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/panama-court-voids-ck-hutchison-port-contracts-2026-01-30/ )

THE COUNTERFACTUAL TEST

Panama's court, auditors, elected officials and commercial operators had their own incentives. Washington publicly favored reducing Chinese-linked influence, but the existence of U.S. pressure does not prove the court would have ruled differently without it. The causal test would compare the audit's origins, the concession's legal vulnerabilities, court reasoning, operator transition planning, shipping-line decisions and U.S. policy actions on a dated timeline. A second test would ask whether a negotiated commercial sale, competitive tender or continued concession with tighter oversight would have preserved more throughput and avoided arbitration exposure.

THE VERDICT SO FAR

Confirmed: Panama replaced a Hong Kong-linked concession holder with temporary European-linked operators; its port network grew while the two transferred terminals lost traffic; and multiple arbitrations and a worker transition remain unresolved. Interpretation: Washington's strategic objective has advanced in the narrow sense that the prior operator lost control. Unknown: whether U.S. cargo became safer or cheaper, whether Panama's long-run net fiscal position improved, how much traffic was permanently diverted, and whether any American economic gain exceeds the local and legal costs.

Until the final concession awards, employment outcomes, fee receipts, legal decisions and comparable U.S. trade-flow data are documented, declaring this a net American victory — or a net Panamanian loss — goes beyond the evidence.
